## Hormonal control across timescales

Peptide hormones bind cell-surface receptors and use second messengers or kinase cascades. Steroid and thyroid hormones cross membranes and regulate gene expression through nuclear receptors. Catecholamines act rapidly through G-protein-coupled receptors. Hormone concentration, receptor abundance, signal amplification, feedback, and clearance jointly shape the response.

The same hormone can produce different effects in different tissues. Glucagon strongly targets liver but not skeletal muscle. Epinephrine mobilizes liver glycogen to support blood glucose and muscle glycogen to support local contraction. Insulin acts broadly but depends on tissue-specific transporters and enzymes.

## Tissue specialization

**Liver** stabilizes blood fuels. It stores glycogen after meals, performs gluconeogenesis during fasting, synthesizes fatty acids and cholesterol when carbon is abundant, oxidizes fatty acids, produces ketone bodies, and converts nitrogen to urea.

**Skeletal muscle** uses glycogen, glucose, fatty acids, and ketone bodies according to intensity and nutritional state. It lacks glucose 6-phosphatase, so its glycogen is local. During intense work it produces lactate and alanine for export.

**Adipose tissue** stores triacylglycerol after feeding and releases fatty acids during fasting. Insulin stimulates GLUT4 translocation and storage; catecholamines stimulate lipolysis.

**Brain** normally relies heavily on glucose and has little fuel storage. During prolonged fasting it adapts to ketone bodies, reducing glucose demand but still requiring some glucose. **Erythrocytes** lack mitochondria and depend completely on glycolysis, releasing lactate.

## Fed state

Rising glucose and incretin signals promote insulin secretion. Insulin increases glucose uptake in muscle and adipose tissue, activates glycogen synthesis, supports glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway, promotes fatty-acid and triacylglycerol synthesis, and favors protein synthesis.

Liver converts excess carbohydrate to fatty acids and exports triacylglycerol in VLDL. Adipose lipoprotein lipase captures fatty acids from chylomicrons and VLDL. Glucagon is low, limiting hepatic glucose production.

## Early fasting

Falling insulin and rising glucagon shift liver from storage to output. Hepatic glycogenolysis initially maintains blood glucose. Adipose lipolysis releases fatty acids and glycerol. Muscle and liver increase fatty-acid oxidation, sparing glucose. Gluconeogenesis rises using lactate, alanine, glycerol, and other substrates.

The Cori cycle moves lactate from anaerobic tissues to liver for glucose synthesis. The glucose-alanine cycle moves both carbon and nitrogen from muscle to liver. These cycles support peripheral metabolism but cost hepatic ATP.

## Prolonged fasting and starvation

Liver glycogen becomes depleted within roughly a day, making gluconeogenesis the main glucose source. High fatty-acid oxidation supplies ATP and acetyl-CoA. Acetyl-CoA activates pyruvate carboxylase, while oxaloacetate is drawn toward gluconeogenesis. Excess acetyl-CoA becomes ketone bodies.

As brain ketone use increases, glucose demand and muscle protein breakdown fall. This is protein sparing, not a complete replacement of glucose: erythrocytes and some biosynthetic needs remain obligately glucose dependent.

## Exercise

Working muscle increases ATP turnover immediately through phosphocreatine, glycolysis, and oxidative phosphorylation. Calcium activates glycogen breakdown and dehydrogenases locally. AMP-activated protein kinase responds to energy stress, increasing fuel uptake and oxidation while suppressing energy-consuming synthesis.

Short intense activity relies more on phosphocreatine and glycolysis; sustained lower-intensity activity relies increasingly on oxidative metabolism and fatty acids. Training changes mitochondrial content, capillary supply, transporters, enzymes, and fuel preference.

## Insulin signaling and diabetes

Insulin receptor activation recruits IRS proteins and phosphoinositide 3-kinase, leading to Akt activation. Akt promotes GLUT4 translocation, glycogen synthesis, lipid synthesis, protein synthesis, and survival while suppressing hepatic gluconeogenic gene expression.

Type 1 diabetes reflects autoimmune beta-cell loss and absolute insulin deficiency. Unrestrained lipolysis and hepatic ketogenesis can cause ketoacidosis. Type 2 diabetes combines insulin resistance with progressive beta-cell dysfunction. Liver may continue glucose production despite high blood glucose, while adipose fatty-acid release and ectopic lipid worsen insulin signaling.

These disorders are not merely failures of glucose uptake; they alter lipid flux, protein metabolism, ketone production, osmotic balance, and vascular biology.

## Adipose tissue as an endocrine organ

Leptin reports energy stores to hypothalamic circuits and influences appetite and energy expenditure. Obesity commonly involves leptin resistance rather than leptin absence. Adiponectin is associated with improved fatty-acid oxidation and insulin sensitivity. Inflammatory cytokines, altered adipose expansion, and ectopic lipid contribute to systemic insulin resistance.

Epinephrine stimulates cAMP-dependent lipolysis. Insulin suppresses lipolysis and promotes re-esterification. The balance between storage capacity and fatty-acid spillover strongly affects liver and muscle metabolism.

## Integration as a control problem

Metabolic homeostasis combines feedforward anticipation, negative feedback, substrate cycles, and tissue exchange. A useful analysis always asks:

1. Which tissue is acting?
2. What is the hormonal and energetic state?
3. Which fuels enter and leave?
4. Which pathways are activated allosterically, covalently, or transcriptionally?
5. Where do carbon, nitrogen, electrons, and ATP ultimately go?

## Takeaways

- Hormones coordinate tissue-specific programs rather than imposing one universal response.
- Feeding favors uptake and storage; fasting favors hepatic glucose output and fatty-acid mobilization.
- Prolonged fasting shifts brain toward ketones and preserves protein.
- Diabetes disrupts integrated carbohydrate, lipid, and protein metabolism, not glucose alone.
