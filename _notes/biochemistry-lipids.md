## Fatty-acid notation and physical properties

Fatty acids are hydrocarbon chains with a carboxyl group. The notation $18:1(\Delta^9)$ describes an 18-carbon chain with one double bond between C9 and C10 when counted from the carboxyl end. Omega notation counts from the methyl end; an omega-3 fatty acid has its first double bond three carbons from that end.

Biological double bonds are usually cis and nonconjugated. A cis bond creates a bend that disrupts packing and lowers melting temperature. Longer chains and greater saturation strengthen dispersion interactions and raise melting temperature. This explains why many saturated fats are solid while unsaturated oils are liquid at room temperature.

Humans cannot introduce double bonds beyond carbon 9 from the carboxyl end, so linoleic acid and alpha-linolenic acid are essential dietary precursors. They support synthesis of longer polyunsaturated fatty acids and lipid mediators.

## Triacylglycerols and waxes

Triacylglycerols esterify three fatty acids to glycerol. They form hydrophobic droplets rather than bilayers and are efficient energy stores for two reasons: their carbons are highly reduced, and they are stored without a large hydration shell. Adipocytes and seeds mobilize them with lipases.

Partial hydrogenation increases oxidative stability but can convert cis bonds to trans bonds. Industrial trans fats adversely alter plasma lipoproteins and cardiovascular risk. Waxes are esters of long-chain fatty acids with long-chain alcohols; their high melting points and water repellency suit protective coatings.

## Glycerophospholipids

Glycerophospholipids contain two fatty acyl chains, glycerol, phosphate, and a polar head group. Phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylinositol, and phosphatidylglycerol differ mainly in head group. Cardiolipin joins two phosphatidate units through glycerol and is abundant in bacterial membranes and the mitochondrial inner membrane.

Some lipids use ether rather than ester linkages. Plasmalogens are enriched in heart and neural tissue. Platelet-activating factor has an ether-linked alkyl chain and a short acetyl group, which contribute to its signaling properties.

## Plant, archaeal, and sphingolipid architectures

Galactolipids and sulfolipids are abundant in chloroplast thylakoids and conserve phosphate. Archaeal lipids use ether-linked isoprenoid chains and opposite glycerol stereochemistry from bacterial and eukaryotic lipids. Some span the membrane as tetraethers, forming a stable monolayer suited to extreme environments.

Sphingolipids use sphingosine rather than glycerol. Addition of a fatty acid produces ceramide, the central precursor. Phosphocholine creates sphingomyelin; carbohydrate head groups create glycosphingolipids and gangliosides. These molecules contribute to myelin, membrane organization, signaling, and cell recognition. Defective lysosomal degradation causes sphingolipid storage diseases.

## Sterols and bile acids

Cholesterol contains a small polar hydroxyl group, a rigid four-ring nucleus, and a hydrocarbon tail. It modulates membrane permeability and fluidity and is the precursor of steroid hormones, bile acids, and vitamin D. Bile salts are amphipathic cholesterol derivatives that emulsify dietary lipids and increase their accessibility to lipases.

## Lipid-derived signals and cofactors

Phosphatidylinositol phosphates organize signaling at membrane surfaces. Cleavage of phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate by phospholipase C generates diacylglycerol and inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate; these activate protein kinase C and release intracellular calcium, respectively.

Eicosanoids act locally in inflammation, vascular tone, platelet function, and smooth muscle. Steroid hormones act through intracellular receptors to regulate transcription. Vitamins A and D are hormone precursors; vitamin E is a lipid-phase antioxidant; vitamin K supports gamma-carboxylation of glutamate residues in clotting proteins. Ubiquinone and related lipid quinones transfer electrons in membranes.

## Lipid analysis

Lipids can be extracted into organic solvents and separated by thin-layer, gas-liquid, or high-performance liquid chromatography. Hydrolysis and derivatization reveal fatty-acid composition. Mass spectrometry identifies molecular species and fragmentation patterns, while isotope labeling traces synthesis and turnover.

## Takeaways

- Chain length, saturation, and cis geometry control lipid packing and melting.
- Storage lipids, bilayer lipids, sterols, and signals use distinct architectures.
- Membrane lipid diversity reflects organism, organelle, and physiological function.
- Lipids are structural molecules, fuels, cofactors, hormones, and local signals.
