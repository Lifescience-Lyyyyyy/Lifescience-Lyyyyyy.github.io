## Innate and adaptive defense

Vaccination began experimentally with Edward Jenner's smallpox work and demonstrated that immune memory can protect before natural exposure. The innate immune system responds rapidly through barriers, phagocytes, complement, cytokines, and pattern-recognition receptors. The adaptive system generates clonally distributed B- and T-cell receptors, expands selected clones, and leaves long-lived memory.

Inflammation produces redness, heat, swelling, and pain because mediators alter blood flow, vascular permeability, and leukocyte recruitment. Chemokines guide migration. Toll-like receptors contain extracellular or luminal leucine-rich repeats and cytosolic signaling domains. TLR4 recognizes bacterial lipopolysaccharide with accessory proteins and activates NF-kappaB and other transcription factors, inducing inflammatory genes.

## B and T lymphocytes

Experiments involving the thymus and the avian bursa of Fabricius separated cellular from antibody-mediated immunity. T cells mature in the thymus; B cells generate antibodies. CD8 T cells recognize peptides presented by MHC class I, which samples proteins made inside most nucleated cells. CD4 T cells recognize peptides on MHC class II, expressed mainly by professional antigen-presenting cells.

Antigen-receptor diversity is created by V(D)J recombination. RAG proteins cut and join variable, diversity, and joining segments. Light chains use V and J segments; heavy chains and T-cell receptor beta chains include D segments. Imprecise joining adds further diversity. Each lymphocyte expresses one dominant receptor specificity, enabling clonal selection.

## Antigen entry and lymph-node organization

Lymph transports soluble and cell-associated antigen into lymph nodes. Subcapsular macrophages can capture complement-opsonized material and display intact antigen to B cells. Dendritic cells carry processed antigen and activate naive T cells. Chemokine receptors position lymphocytes in B-cell follicles or T-cell zones, increasing the probability that rare cognate cells meet.

After antigen binding and appropriate T-cell help, activated B cells can become short-lived plasmablasts or enter germinal centers. Early IgM is followed by class-switched antibodies such as IgG, whose constant region changes effector function without changing the selected antigen-binding site.

## Germinal-center selection

Germinal centers divide into functional dark and light zones. In the dark zone, proliferating B cells express activation-induced cytidine deaminase. AID converts cytidine to uridine in transcribed immunoglobulin genes, initiating somatic hypermutation and class-switch recombination.

Mutated cells then test their receptors in the light zone by competing for antigen held on follicular dendritic cells. Cells that capture antigen efficiently present more peptide–MHC II to follicular-helper T cells and receive survival and proliferation signals. Poor competitors die. Selected cells may re-enter the dark zone for another mutation cycle, differentiate into antibody-secreting plasma cells, or become memory B cells. This iterative mutation–selection process produces affinity maturation.

Linked recognition ensures specificity: a B cell internalizes antigen through its receptor and presents peptides from that same molecular complex to a helper T cell. T-cell help therefore confirms that antibody binding is connected to a peptide recognized by the adaptive response.

## Takeaways

- Innate receptors detect conserved danger patterns; adaptive receptors are generated clonally.
- V(D)J recombination builds receptor diversity before antigen exposure.
- Lymph-node organization brings rare antigen-specific cells together.
- Germinal centers improve antibodies through AID-driven mutation and T-cell-dependent selection.
