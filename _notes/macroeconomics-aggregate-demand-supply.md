## Time horizons and potential output

The long run is not a fixed number of months. It is the horizon over which wages, prices, expectations, and contracts have adjusted so output returns to potential. Potential GDP is production consistent with normal utilization and the natural rate of unemployment; it is not a physical maximum. The output gap is

$$
\text{gap}=100\times\frac{Y-Y^*}{Y^*}.
$$

A negative gap accompanies cyclical unemployment; a positive gap indicates temporarily high utilization and inflation pressure.

## Aggregate demand

Aggregate demand describes combinations of the price level and real output for which goods and asset markets are jointly consistent. A higher price level lowers real money balances, tends to raise interest rates, reduces interest-sensitive spending, and may appreciate the currency, reducing net exports. Monetary or fiscal expansion, optimism, foreign growth, or easier credit shifts AD right; the opposite shifts it left.

Keynes emphasized insufficient effective demand during the Great Depression. Monetarists highlighted the role of money and financial conditions. Modern analysis combines expenditure, policy rules, expectations, and financial frictions.

## Long-run aggregate supply

Long-run aggregate supply is vertical at potential output because long-run production depends on labor, capital, natural resources, technology, and institutions rather than the price level. Population and participation, capital formation, resource access, productivity, and allocation shift LRAS.

A horizontal supply curve is a limiting case with extensive idle resources and constant marginal cost. A vertical curve is a long-run limiting case. The upward-sloping SRAS between them reflects incomplete nominal adjustment.

## Microfoundations of SRAS

Sticky nominal wages arise from contracts, bargaining, morale, insurance, and adjustment cost. If prices rise unexpectedly while wages are fixed, real labor cost falls and firms expand production. Sticky product prices arise from menu costs, customer relationships, coordination, and strategic complementarity; firms whose prices lag behind an expansion experience higher demand. Imperfect information can cause producers to confuse an aggregate price change with a change in their relative price.

A common representation is

$$
Y=Y^*+\alpha(P-P^e), \qquad \alpha>0.
$$

Output exceeds potential when the price level is unexpectedly high. Higher expected prices shift SRAS upward or left because wages and other costs are set higher.

## Demand shocks

A negative demand shock lowers output and prices in the short run. Unemployment rises and, over time, wage and price growth weakens. SRAS shifts down/right until output returns to potential at a lower price path. A positive demand shock produces the reverse. In the basic model, demand affects real output temporarily but the long-run price level permanently.

The speed and cost of self-correction depend on debt, deflation risk, expectations, hysteresis, financial stress, and nominal rigidity. A deep recession can damage labor attachment and investment, so potential output itself may fall.

## Supply shocks

A negative supply shock—such as a sharp input-cost increase or disruption—shifts SRAS left, raising prices while lowering output: stagflation. Stabilizing both variables simultaneously is difficult. Accommodating demand supports output but raises inflation; contraction lowers inflation but deepens the recession. A positive productivity or supply shock raises output and lowers price pressure.

Supply expectations can generate wage–price persistence, but an endless spiral is not automatic. Credible policy, slack, contract duration, bargaining institutions, import prices, and productivity determine propagation.

## Takeaways

- Potential output is a sustainable benchmark, not a hard production ceiling.
- LRAS reflects real productive capacity; SRAS reflects incomplete nominal adjustment.
- Demand shocks move output and prices in the same direction; supply shocks move them in opposite directions.
- Stagflation creates a genuine stabilization-policy trade-off.
