## What GDP measures

Gross domestic product is the market value of final goods and services produced within a geographic area during a specified period. “Domestic” uses location rather than ownership; “gross” means depreciation has not been subtracted. Simon Kuznets developed national-income measures during the Great Depression, and wartime planning helped establish government purchases within modern accounts.

Gross national income adds net primary income received from abroad:

$$
\mathrm{GNI}=\mathrm{GDP}+\text{income received from abroad}-\text{income paid abroad}.
$$

## Circular flow and three equivalent approaches

Production creates income and the sale of production creates expenditure. In a complete accounting system, the production, expenditure, and income approaches therefore measure the same activity.

The expenditure identity is

$$
Y=C+I+G+NX,
$$

where consumption (C) is household spending except newly produced housing; investment (I) includes business fixed capital, inventories, and new residential construction; government purchases (G) exclude pure transfers; and net exports (NX=X-M). Imports are subtracted because they may already appear in (C), (I), or (G) but were not produced domestically.

The identity is ex post accounting, not a behavioral theory. A household purchase of existing shares or an existing house transfers ownership and is not current production. Brokerage services and new construction are counted. Unsold output enters inventory investment, preserving the equality between production and expenditure.

The production approach sums value added, avoiding double counting of intermediate goods. The income approach sums labor compensation, production taxes net of subsidies, depreciation, and operating surplus. Net domestic product is GDP minus depreciation.

## Saving identities

Disposable income can be allocated to consumption and saving. With government and foreign sectors,

$$
S_{\mathrm{national}}=Y-C-G=I+NX.
$$

Private saving is disposable income minus consumption; public saving is taxes minus government purchases and transfers. In a closed economy (NX=0), so national saving equals domestic investment. In an open economy the difference is matched by net foreign investment.

## Nominal and real GDP

Nominal GDP values current production at current prices. Real GDP holds prices fixed or uses chain-weighted volume indexes to isolate quantity change. The GDP deflator is

$$
\text{GDP deflator}=100\times\frac{\text{nominal GDP}}{\text{real GDP}}.
$$

Unlike a consumer price index, it covers domestically produced final output and its basket changes with production. Growth in per-capita real GDP approximately equals real-GDP growth minus population growth when rates are small.

## Related income concepts and welfare limits

Net national income subtracts depreciation from GNI. Personal income adjusts national income for retained corporate earnings, corporate taxes, social-insurance contributions, transfers, and other flows. Disposable personal income subtracts personal taxes.

GDP is valuable for measuring production and cyclical conditions, but it is not a complete measure of welfare. It omits leisure, household production, much informal activity, distribution, environmental depletion, health, security, and many quality changes. Destruction followed by repair can raise measured production while leaving wealth lower—the economic lesson behind the broken-window fallacy. GDP per capita should therefore be read alongside distributional, environmental, health, and wealth accounts.

## Takeaways

- GDP is domestic production, not wealth or welfare.
- Production, income, and expenditure are three views of the same circular flow.
- The saving–investment identity is accounting; behavioral models explain how equilibrium is reached.
- Real GDP separates quantity growth from price change.
