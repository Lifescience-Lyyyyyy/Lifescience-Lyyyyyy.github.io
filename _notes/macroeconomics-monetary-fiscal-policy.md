## Monetary-policy transmission

Central banks steer financial conditions through policy rates, reserve remuneration, open-market operations, lending facilities, communication, and regulation. A lower expected path of short rates tends to reduce longer yields, relax credit conditions, raise asset prices, weaken the currency, and stimulate consumption, housing, investment, and net exports. The strength of each channel depends on balance sheets, bank capitalization, borrower risk, and expectations.

The downward slope of aggregate demand can be read through this mechanism: a higher price level reduces real money balances and, given policy, is associated with tighter financial conditions and lower real expenditure. A monetary easing shifts AD outward when it lowers relevant real rates or credit spreads.

## Policy near the effective lower bound

When short nominal rates approach their lower bound, central banks can purchase longer-term or risky assets, lend against collateral, guide expectations, or alter the composition of their balance sheets. Quantitative easing may reduce term and liquidity premia, support market functioning, and signal low future policy rates.

Expected inflation also matters through (r=i-\pi^e). If nominal rates cannot fall, a decline in expected inflation raises the real rate and tightens policy. Credible avoidance of deflation can therefore be stimulative. QE is not simply “printing money”: its effect depends on which assets are purchased, market segmentation, fiscal backing, and whether banks or investors change behavior.

## Monetary policy and financial stability

Asset bubbles are difficult to identify in real time. “Cleaning up” after a crash avoids suppressing productive booms but may encourage leverage and moral hazard. “Leaning against the wind” can restrain risk but may require large output costs. Macroprudential tools—capital buffers, loan-to-value limits, stress tests, liquidity rules, and resolution policy—target resilience more directly, while the policy rate addresses broad inflation and demand conditions.

Deleveraging can occur through repayment, default or restructuring, slower credit growth, inflation of nominal GDP, or real growth. Rapid simultaneous deleveraging may depress spending and asset prices, raising debt ratios despite private attempts to reduce them.

## Rules and inflation targets

A Taylor-type rule links the nominal policy rate to inflation and economic slack:

$$
i_t=r^*+\pi_t+\phi_\pi(\pi_t-\pi^*)+\phi_y\tilde y_t.
$$

It is a benchmark, not a mechanical law. Estimates of the neutral real rate and output gap are uncertain. Inflation targeting instead organizes forecasts, communication, accountability, and instrument choice around a numerical objective, usually with flexibility for output and financial stability.

## Fiscal accounts

Let taxes be (T), government purchases (G), and transfers (TR). The primary budget balance is

$$
PB=T-G-TR.
$$

Interest payments must be added for the overall balance. A deficit is a flow; debt is the accumulated stock. The cyclical deficit arises because recession lowers taxes and raises transfers. The structural balance estimates what would remain near potential output and is sensitive to the estimated gap and temporary revenue.

## Multipliers and crowding out

Government purchases enter demand directly. Tax cuts and transfers work through disposable income, so households may save part. Multipliers tend to be larger when resources are idle, monetary policy accommodates, imports are low, credit constraints bind, and spending is timely. They are smaller when policy raises interest rates, exchange rates, imports, or expected future taxes.

Crowding out occurs when fiscal expansion raises interest rates or absorbs scarce resources, reducing private investment or net exports. At the lower bound with slack and accommodative monetary policy, crowding out may be small; near capacity it can be large. Productive public investment can also crowd in private investment by raising future returns.

## Automatic stabilizers

Progressive taxes fall automatically when income falls, while unemployment and means-tested transfers rise. These responses support disposable income without a new legislative decision and reverse during expansions. Automatic stabilizers are timely and predictable, but their size reflects prior policy choices and may be insufficient for unusually large shocks.

## Takeaways

- Monetary policy works through expected real rates, credit, assets, and exchange rates.
- Unconventional policy changes longer yields and risk premia when short rates are constrained.
- Fiscal multipliers depend on slack, financing, openness, composition, and monetary response.
- Automatic stabilizers respond quickly; discretionary policy can be larger but faces design and implementation lags.
