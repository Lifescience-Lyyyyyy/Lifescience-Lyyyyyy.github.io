## What counts as money

Money is an asset generally accepted in payment for goods, services, and debts. Its monetary status depends on acceptability and liquidity, not on physical form. Commodity money has intrinsic nonmonetary use; commodity-backed money is redeemable for an underlying asset; fiat money is accepted through institutions, law, convention, and confidence rather than redemption.

Central-bank currency is a liability of the central bank, while commercial-bank deposits are liabilities of banks. A loan is an asset of the bank and a liability of the borrower; it is not itself money, although lending commonly creates a matching deposit. Government deposits at the central bank are usually excluded from money held by the public.

An asset such as Bitcoin can serve some monetary functions for some users, but volatile purchasing power and limited unit-of-account use make classification context dependent. “Virtual currency,” “cryptoasset,” legal tender, and money are not interchangeable legal or economic categories.

## Four functions

- **Medium of exchange:** money separates sale from purchase and removes the need for a double coincidence of wants.
- **Means of final settlement:** payment extinguishes an obligation within the accepted system.
- **Unit of account:** prices expressed in one unit reduce information and calculation cost.
- **Store of value:** money transfers purchasing power through time, although inflation erodes it.

Credit cards provide payment instructions and credit but are not net money balances; settlement ultimately uses deposits or central-bank liabilities.

## Monetary aggregates

Aggregates order assets by liquidity, but definitions differ by country. A typical hierarchy is currency outside banks, narrow money adding transaction deposits, and broader money adding savings or time deposits and selected market instruments. The labels M0, M1, M2, and beyond are therefore not internationally identical.

Aggregate growth can contain information about transactions and portfolio preference, but a simple M1–M2 “scissors gap” is not a structural law. Financial innovation, regulation, interest on deposits, digital payment, and reclassification can change the relationship.

## Short-run money demand and interest rates

Holding money sacrifices interest available on less liquid assets. Real money demand can be summarized as

$$
\frac{M^d}{P}=L(Y,i), \qquad L_Y>0,\quad L_i<0,
$$

where higher real activity increases transaction demand and a higher nominal interest rate increases the opportunity cost. With sticky prices, an increase in nominal money raises real balances, pushes down short-term interest rates, and can stimulate interest-sensitive expenditure.

Bond prices and yields move inversely. Excess money used to buy bonds raises bond prices and lowers yields until asset holders willingly hold the available money. Central banks today often implement policy by setting or steering a short-term rate while supplying reserves elastically at that target, rather than fixing a vertical money-supply quantity.

## Liquidity traps and the long run

At very low rates, money and short-term safe assets become close substitutes. Additional reserves may be held without much further reduction in the relevant borrowing rates—a liquidity trap. Policy can still act through expectations, longer-maturity purchases, credit spreads, exchange rates, and fiscal–monetary interaction.

In the long run, prices and wages adjust. A permanent rise in nominal money need not permanently lower the real interest rate or raise real output; it primarily changes nominal prices. This distinction between short-run nonneutrality and long-run neutrality is a benchmark, not an assertion that adjustment is instantaneous or costless.

## Takeaways

- Money is defined by function and general acceptability, not by intrinsic value.
- Bank deposits are money liabilities created within a balance-sheet system.
- Monetary aggregates are country-specific liquidity groupings.
- Money affects real activity in the short run when prices, expectations, or financial conditions adjust slowly.
