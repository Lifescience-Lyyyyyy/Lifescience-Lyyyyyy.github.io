## Openness and international accounts

Trade openness is often summarized by ((X+M)/Y), although country size, geography, and global value chains affect the ratio. Goods, services, primary income, transfers, and financial claims connect national economies; gross flows can be large even when net balances are small.

The current account records trade in goods and services, primary income, and secondary income. The capital account records capital transfers and nonproduced nonfinancial assets. The financial account records direct investment, portfolio investment, other investment, derivatives, and reserve assets. Exact signs depend on the statistical presentation, so economic interpretation should follow asset and liability changes rather than memorizing “debit” and “credit” without context.

With consistent conventions, the accounts satisfy

$$
CA+KA+FA+EO=0,
$$

where (EO) is errors and omissions. In the simplified national-income framework,

$$
NX=S-I=NCO,
$$

so a current-account surplus corresponds to net acquisition of foreign assets, while a deficit is financed by net foreign borrowing. This is an accounting relationship, not a claim that one side mechanically causes the other.

## Nominal and real exchange rates

Let (E) be domestic currency per unit of foreign currency. An increase in (E) is a domestic-currency depreciation. If (P^\*) is the foreign price level and (P) the domestic price level, define

$$
q=\frac{EP^*}{P}.
$$

A rise in (q) is a real depreciation: foreign goods become expensive relative to domestic goods, tending to improve domestic price competitiveness. Alternative textbooks invert the definition, so conclusions must follow the stated convention.

## Purchasing-power parity

Absolute purchasing-power parity predicts (E=P/P^\*) when identical tradable baskets have one price. Relative PPP predicts approximately

$$
\frac{\Delta E}{E}\approx \pi-\pi^*.
$$

PPP is a long-run anchor, not an exact short-run law. Transport cost, tariffs, nontradable services, product differentiation, pricing to market, taxes, and sticky prices produce persistent deviations. Balassa–Samuelson effects can create systematic real-exchange-rate differences as productivity changes.

## Interest parity and capital flows

Uncovered interest parity compares expected domestic-currency returns:

$$
i\approx i^*+\frac{E^e_{t+1}-E_t}{E_t}+\rho,
$$

where (
ho) is a risk or liquidity premium. Covered interest parity replaces the expected future spot rate with a forward rate and is a tighter no-arbitrage condition when funding and counterparty constraints are small.

Capital flows respond to expected return, risk, liquidity, regulation, taxes, and hedging cost—not to interest differences alone. They influence loanable funds, exchange rates, asset prices, and external balance simultaneously.

## Exchange-rate regimes and the trilemma

Under a floating rate, market trading determines the exchange rate, although central-bank policy and intervention matter. A managed float permits discretionary intervention. A fixed or pegged rate requires the authority to buy or sell reserves, adjust domestic policy, impose capital controls, or eventually change the peg.

The policy trilemma states that an economy cannot simultaneously maintain a fixed exchange rate, free capital mobility, and an independent monetary policy. It can choose at most two. Fixed rates reduce exchange-rate uncertainty and can anchor inflation, but they import foreign monetary conditions and may become vulnerable when domestic objectives conflict with the peg.

## Takeaways

- International accounts record offsetting real and financial transactions.
- Net exports equal the saving–investment gap in the simplified accounts.
- Real exchange rates combine nominal rates with relative price levels.
- PPP and interest parity are benchmark conditions whose deviations carry economic information.
- The open-economy trilemma constrains exchange-rate and monetary-policy choices.
