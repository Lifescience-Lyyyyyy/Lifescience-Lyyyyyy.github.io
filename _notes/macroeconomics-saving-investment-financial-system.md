## Connecting saving to investment

The financial system transfers purchasing power from savers with funds to borrowers with productive opportunities. Direct finance uses traded claims such as stocks and bonds; indirect finance passes through intermediaries such as banks or mutual funds. Asset prices are forward-looking valuations, while GDP records current production. A newly built house is current investment; trading an existing house or share mainly transfers ownership.

In a closed economy, national saving equals investment. In the loanable-funds model, the real interest rate is the price that balances the supply of saving with the demand for investment. Policies that change public saving, tax returns, risk, or expected profitability shift the curves rather than mechanically setting a fixed quantity.

## Equity markets

A share is a residual ownership claim. An initial public offering sells new shares in the primary market; later trading occurs in secondary markets, which provide liquidity and price discovery but usually do not directly transfer proceeds to the issuing firm. Firms benefit indirectly through the cost of future issuance, employee compensation, collateral, acquisitions, and shares they retain.

Earnings per share are earnings divided by shares outstanding. The price–earnings ratio compares price with annual earnings per share and reflects expected growth, risk, interest rates, accounting, and sentiment. Dividend yield is dividends per share divided by price—not dividends divided by retained earnings. The bid–ask spread compensates liquidity providers and measures one component of trading cost.

## Debt and present value

A bond promises coupons and principal. Its price is the present value of future payments:

$$
P=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t}+\frac{F}{(1+r)^T}.
$$

Bond prices therefore fall when required yields rise. Longer maturity generally increases interest-rate sensitivity. Default risk raises required yield, while favorable tax treatment can lower it.

Debt has priority and a fixed obligation; equity absorbs residual gains and losses. Debt can preserve ownership and offer tax advantages but increases insolvency risk. Equity provides loss-absorbing capital but dilutes control and demands disclosure. The optimal mix depends on cash-flow volatility, information, taxes, agency costs, and financial distress—not simply whether business conditions are good.

## Mutual funds and diversification

Mutual funds pool household resources into portfolios. Scale lowers trading and research cost; specialization delegates management; diversification reduces idiosyncratic risk. Open-end funds issue and redeem shares at net asset value, whereas closed-end shares trade in a secondary market.

Fund shares fluctuate with asset values, so ordinary funds do not promise a fixed redemption value. They can still face liquidity stress when investors redeem quickly and underlying assets are illiquid.

## Banks and fragility

Banks fund longer-term, risky, illiquid loans with short-term, liquid deposits. They screen borrowers, monitor loans, transform maturity, provide payment services, and create deposit money. This balance-sheet structure supports credit but creates fragility. Loan losses reduce bank equity while deposit obligations remain. If depositors run, a solvent but illiquid bank may be forced into fire sales.

Deposit insurance, lender-of-last-resort facilities, capital and liquidity requirements, supervision, and resolution regimes reduce systemic risk. They can also weaken market discipline and encourage risk taking, creating a policy trade-off between stability and moral hazard.

## Takeaways

- Finance reallocates saving across time, projects, and risk bearers.
- Secondary-market liquidity supports primary financing even when trades do not fund firms directly.
- Bond value is discounted cash flow, so price and yield move inversely.
- Banks create useful liquidity through maturity transformation, which also makes them vulnerable to runs.
