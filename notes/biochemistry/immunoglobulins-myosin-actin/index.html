Biochemistry / Chapter 6

Antibody architecture

An immunoglobulin contains two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains joined by disulfides. Each chain contains immunoglobulin-fold domains. The variable domains of one heavy and one light chain meet to form an antigen-binding site, so one conventional antibody has two equivalent sites.

Proteolysis reveals the functional organization. Papain produces two Fab fragments, which bind antigen, and one Fc fragment, which recruits immune effector systems. The hinge provides flexibility so the two Fab arms can engage epitopes at different separations.

Recognition and effector function

Antibodies recognize molecular surfaces through shape complementarity and many noncovalent contacts. Affinity refers to one binding interaction; avidity describes the combined strength of multiple contacts. Even moderate single-site affinity can produce strong multivalent attachment.

The major human classes differ in heavy-chain constant regions:

  • IgM is produced early and forms a high-avidity pentamer in secretion;
  • IgG dominates blood and extracellular fluid and can cross the placenta;
  • IgA protects mucosal surfaces and is often secreted as a dimer;
  • IgE binds mast cells and participates in defense against parasites and allergic responses;
  • IgD is mainly a B-cell receptor.

Fc regions engage Fc receptors and complement, enabling neutralization, opsonization, complement activation, and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity.

Generating antibody diversity

Developing B cells assemble variable regions by V(D)J recombination. Imprecise joining creates additional junctional diversity, and different heavy and light chains pair combinatorially. After antigen stimulation, somatic hypermutation and selection increase affinity, while class-switch recombination changes the constant region without changing antigen specificity.

Polyclonal serum contains antibodies from many B-cell clones and recognizes multiple epitopes. A monoclonal antibody derives from one clone and has defined specificity. Hybridoma technology, recombinant expression, humanization, and display libraries turn this principle into diagnostic and therapeutic reagents.

Actin and myosin organization

G-actin monomers polymerize into polar F-actin filaments. Myosin II contains two motor heads, neck regions with light chains, and a long coiled-coil tail. Many myosin molecules assemble into bipolar thick filaments. In striated muscle, thin and thick filaments interdigitate within sarcomeres bounded by Z discs.

Titin spans from the Z disc toward the thick filament and provides elasticity and alignment. Nebulin associates with thin filaments and contributes to their organization. The repeated sarcomere architecture converts nanometre-scale motor motion into macroscopic contraction.

The cross-bridge cycle

The mechanical cycle couples ATP chemistry to changes in actin affinity:

  1. ATP binding lowers myosin’s affinity for actin and causes detachment.
  2. ATP hydrolysis primes the head; ADP and phosphate remain bound.
  3. The primed head binds a new position on actin.
  4. Phosphate release strengthens binding and triggers the power stroke.
  5. ADP leaves, producing a strongly bound rigor state until another ATP binds.

ATP is therefore required for detachment as well as force generation. After death, ATP depletion traps actomyosin complexes and produces rigor mortis.

Calcium regulation

At rest, tropomyosin covers myosin-binding sites on actin. Calcium released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum binds troponin C, rearranging the troponin complex and moving tropomyosin. Myosin can then engage actin. Calcium reuptake terminates contraction.

This system illustrates a recurring design: a chemical signal changes a regulatory protein, which exposes a binding site and permits an ATP-driven machine to operate.

Takeaways

  • Antibodies separate antigen recognition in Fab regions from effector recruitment through Fc.
  • Gene rearrangement and somatic selection create vast specificity from a finite genome.
  • Actin and myosin form an ATP-driven cyclic motor rather than a static contractile link.
  • Calcium controls access to actin, coupling electrical excitation to mechanical work.
