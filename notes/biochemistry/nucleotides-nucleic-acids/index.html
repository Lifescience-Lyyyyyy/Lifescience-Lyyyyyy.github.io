Biochemistry / Chapter 11

Nucleotides as multifunctional molecules

A nucleoside contains a nitrogenous base linked to ribose or deoxyribose; a nucleotide adds one or more phosphate groups. Purines are adenine and guanine. Pyrimidines are cytosine, thymine, and uracil. The base attaches through an N-glycosidic bond to C1’ of the sugar, and phosphates commonly attach at C5’.

Nucleotides are not only nucleic-acid monomers. ATP and GTP transfer free energy and phosphate; NAD, FAD, and coenzyme A contain nucleotide-derived modules; cAMP and cGMP act as second messengers; and activated intermediates such as UDP-glucose transfer chemical groups.

Chemical stability and base modification

Bases can adopt rare tautomeric forms with altered hydrogen-bonding patterns, creating replication errors if not corrected. Deamination converts cytosine to uracil and can convert 5-methylcytosine to thymine. Spontaneous depurination cleaves the glycosidic bond. DNA repair systems are therefore essential even without external mutagens.

Methylation and other base modifications carry regulatory information. CpG methylation in vertebrates is associated with chromatin regulation and mutational hotspots. RNA contains many modified bases that tune folding, decoding, and stability.

Phosphodiester backbone and directionality

Nucleotides in DNA and RNA are joined by 3’-5’ phosphodiester bonds. The backbone has polarity, so sequences are written 5’ to 3’. At physiological pH the phosphates are negatively charged, requiring counterions and positively charged proteins for compact folding.

RNA has a 2’-hydroxyl group. This enables additional hydrogen bonding and catalysis but also permits base-catalyzed backbone cleavage through intramolecular attack. DNA lacks the 2’-hydroxyl and is chemically more stable for long-term information storage.

DNA double helix

Watson-Crick pairing places adenine with thymine and guanine with cytosine. Hydrogen bonding provides specificity, while base stacking and hydrophobic burial contribute strongly to stability. Antiparallel strands form a right-handed B-DNA helix under common physiological conditions, with major and minor grooves exposing distinct chemical patterns for protein recognition.

A-DNA is shorter and wider and is favored by dehydrated DNA and many RNA duplexes. Z-DNA is left-handed and can form in particular sequences and ionic conditions. DNA can also form hairpins, cruciforms, triplexes, G-quadruplexes, and supercoiled structures.

Melting and hybridization

Heating or extreme pH disrupts base pairing and stacking, increasing absorbance near 260 nm: the hyperchromic effect. The melting temperature rises with GC content, ionic strength, length, and sequence complementarity. Mismatches lower duplex stability.

The transition is cooperative because disrupting one region makes neighboring disruption easier. Hybridization specificity underlies PCR primers, probes, sequencing, and many diagnostic assays.

RNA structure and catalysis

RNA is usually single stranded but folds through local complementarity into stems, loops, bulges, pseudoknots, and tertiary contacts. Metal ions often stabilize compact folds. tRNA combines a conserved secondary structure with an L-shaped tertiary structure; rRNA forms the structural and catalytic core of the ribosome.

Ribozymes demonstrate that RNA can catalyze phosphodiester cleavage and ligation. Catalysis may use acid-base groups, metal ions, electrostatic organization, and precise positioning. The ribosome’s peptidyl-transferase center is primarily RNA, linking information storage to catalysis.

Takeaways

  • Nucleotide chemistry supports information storage, energy transfer, signaling, and group transfer.
  • DNA stability comes from both specific pairing and extensive base stacking.
  • Groove geometry allows proteins to read sequence without opening the helix.
  • RNA’s 2’-hydroxyl makes it less stable but expands its structural and catalytic repertoire.
