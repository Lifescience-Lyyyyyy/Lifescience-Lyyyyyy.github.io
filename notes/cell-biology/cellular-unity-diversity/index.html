Cell Biology / Chapter 1

Cell theory and common cellular logic

Cell theory rests on three claims: every organism contains one or more cells; the cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life; and every cell descends from a pre-existing cell. Cells differ enormously in size, shape, metabolism, and specialization, yet all use a selectively permeable membrane, DNA as hereditary material, RNA and ribosomes for gene expression, and related pathways for energy conversion.

The three domains—Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya—were resolved by comparing conserved ribosomal RNA sequences. Archaea are prokaryotic in cellular organization but differ from bacteria in membrane chemistry and in many components of their information-processing machinery. Biochemical diversity is greatest among prokaryotes: different species can use light, inorganic chemicals, or organic compounds as energy sources and can exploit electron donors and acceptors unavailable to animals or plants.

Genomes, conservation, and innovation

Evolution changes different genes at different rates. Components of translation and other essential systems are strongly conserved, whereas genes involved in environmental interaction often evolve rapidly. Gene duplication supplies raw material for innovation: one copy can retain the ancestral function while the other accumulates changes, creating a family of homologous proteins with related but specialized roles.

More than two hundred gene families are shared by all three domains, supporting descent from a common ancestral population. A minimal comparison of these ancient families emphasizes metabolism, information processing, and core cellular functions. Genome size, however, does not directly measure organismal complexity. The human genome has far more DNA than a bacterial genome, but much of it is noncoding and complexity also arises from regulation, alternative RNA processing, and combinatorial protein interactions.

Origin of the eukaryotic cell

Eukaryotic internal membranes probably arose partly through invagination and specialization of an ancestral plasma membrane. Mitochondria originated from an engulfed aerobic bacterium; chloroplasts later arose from a photosynthetic cyanobacterium. Evidence includes double membranes, bacterial-type ribosomes, circular genomes, division by fission, and phylogenetic relationships between organelle and bacterial genes. Extensive gene transfer to the nucleus made organelles dependent on proteins synthesized in the cytosol.

Why model organisms matter

Organism Experimental strengths Representative discoveries
Escherichia coli Rapid growth and a compact genome DNA replication, transcription, translation, and the genetic code
Budding yeast A simple eukaryote with powerful genetics Cell-cycle control and organelle biology
Arabidopsis thaliana Small plant genome and short generation time Plant development and signaling
Caenorhabditis elegans Invariant lineage of 959 adult somatic cells Programmed cell death and neural development
Drosophila melanogaster Sophisticated genetics and conserved disease genes Pattern formation and chromosome biology
Frog and zebrafish Accessible vertebrate embryos Vertebrate development and organogenesis

Conservation makes these systems informative; differences reveal how a common cellular toolkit has been adapted.

Takeaways

  • All cells share a core molecular architecture inherited from common ancestry.
  • Gene conservation preserves essential functions, while duplication and divergence create novelty.
  • Endosymbiosis explains the bacterial ancestry of mitochondria and chloroplasts.
  • Model organisms expose conserved mechanisms in experimentally tractable settings.
