Cell Biology / Chapter 4

Three filament systems

System Typical diameter Principal roles
Actin filaments 7 nm Cell cortex, protrusion, contraction, cytokinesis, microvilli
Intermediate filaments about 10 nm Tensile strength and mechanical continuity
Microtubules 25 nm Organelle positioning, long-range transport, cilia, mitotic spindle

All three are assemblies of protein subunits, but their architecture gives them different mechanics. Actin and microtubules are polar and dynamic. Intermediate filaments are rope-like, nonpolar, and especially resistant to stretching.

Actin assembly and organization

Actin monomers bind ATP and assemble head-to-tail. Nucleation is slow, after which elongation is faster at the barbed plus end than at the pointed minus end. ATP hydrolysis lowers subunit affinity within older filament regions. At suitable monomer concentrations, addition at the plus end and loss at the minus end produce treadmilling.

Cells control the available monomer pool and filament geometry. Thymosin sequesters monomers, whereas profilin promotes exchange and addition. Formins nucleate and remain attached to straight, unbranched filaments. Arp2/3 nucleates branched networks that push lamellipodia forward. Cofilin twists and severs ADP-rich filament regions, accelerating turnover. Cross-linkers create tight parallel bundles, contractile antiparallel bundles, or orthogonal gels.

Myosin motors convert ATP hydrolysis into movement on actin. Myosin II slides antiparallel filaments in muscle, stress fibers, and the cytokinetic ring. Myosin V carries cargo along actin cables. Nonmuscle myosin II is commonly regulated by phosphorylation of its light chain rather than by the troponin system.

Microtubule dynamics and motors

A microtubule usually contains thirteen protofilaments built from alpha/beta-tubulin heterodimers. Beta-tubulin hydrolyzes its exchangeable GTP after assembly. A GTP-rich cap favors a straight, growing end; loss of the cap exposes curved GDP-tubulin and triggers catastrophe. Rescue returns a shrinking end to growth. This dynamic instability allows rapid exploration of cellular space.

Gamma-tubulin ring complexes nucleate microtubules at organizing centers such as the centrosome. Microtubule-associated proteins stabilize, space, sever, or track growing ends. Kinesins generally move toward plus ends and dyneins toward minus ends, transporting organelles, RNA, and vesicles. Ciliary dynein instead drives sliding between axonemal microtubules.

Intermediate filaments and cell mechanics

Intermediate-filament proteins form coiled-coil dimers, staggered tetramers, and rope-like filaments. Keratins reinforce epithelia, vimentin-family proteins support mesenchymal cells, neurofilaments strengthen axons, and lamins support the nuclear envelope. Keratin mutations can cause epidermolysis bullosa simplex because fragile basal cells rupture under modest stress. SUN–KASH complexes mechanically couple cytoplasmic filaments to the nuclear lamina.

Polarity and migration

Migrating cells coordinate protrusion, adhesion, contraction, and rear release. Rho-family GTPases organize this polarity: Cdc42 helps establish a front, Rac stimulates branched actin and lamellipodia, and RhoA promotes formin-dependent bundles, myosin contraction, and focal adhesions. Chemotactic cues bias these feedback circuits rather than specifying every filament.

Takeaways

  • Cytoskeletal behavior emerges from regulated polymer dynamics and accessory proteins.
  • Actin supports cortical shape and force; microtubules organize long-range transport; intermediate filaments resist tension.
  • Nucleotide hydrolysis makes actin and microtubules dynamic rather than passive scaffolds.
  • Cell migration is a coordinated cycle controlled by polarity signaling and mechanical feedback.
