Macroeconomics / Chapter 3

Measuring inequality

Income shares compare the resources received by quantiles of the population. Common summaries include the top 1 or 10 percent share, the ratio of the top to bottom quintile, and the Palma ratio—the top 10 percent share divided by the bottom 40 percent share.

The Lorenz curve plots cumulative population, ordered from poorest to richest, against cumulative income. The Gini coefficient equals the area between perfect equality and the Lorenz curve divided by the entire area below the equality line. In a continuous representation,

\[G = 1-2\int_0^1 L(p)\,dp.\]

(G=0) denotes complete equality and (G=1) the limiting case in which one person receives everything. The coefficient compresses the whole distribution into one number, so different distributions can have the same Gini value. Survey nonresponse, missing top incomes, household-size adjustment, taxes, transfers, and regional aggregation can materially change estimates.

Poverty and the unit of measurement

The poverty rate is the share of people below a chosen poverty line. An absolute line represents a fixed real standard; a relative line is defined with reference to contemporary median income; a weakly relative approach combines both ideas. The poverty gap measures how far poor households fall below the line and therefore distinguishes shallow from deep poverty.

International comparisons require purchasing-power conversion. Income surveys and consumption surveys are not interchangeable: borrowing and dissaving let low-income households consume more than current income, while high-income households save a larger share. Consumption can better track permanent resources but may understate inequality at the top.

Annual cash income omits taxes, tax credits, in-kind benefits, household production, public services, and wealth. It also mixes persistent inequality with temporary life-cycle fluctuations. A student, a temporarily unemployed worker, and a person facing permanent exclusion can have the same annual income but very different prospects.

Mobility

Mobility describes movement across income ranks over time. Short spells of poverty are more common than long-term poverty, but persistent deprivation has greater cumulative effects. Intergenerational mobility asks how strongly children’s outcomes depend on parental outcomes. High inequality is often associated with lower mobility, although institutions, education, health, residential segregation, and family structure mediate the relationship.

Competing ethical frameworks

  • Utilitarianism favors policies that maximize total utility. Diminishing marginal utility supports redistribution, but incentive costs limit complete equalization.
  • Liberal or Rawlsian reasoning evaluates rules behind a veil of ignorance. The maximin principle emphasizes the welfare of the least advantaged.
  • Libertarianism emphasizes voluntary exchange, property rights, and procedural fairness; a distribution can be legitimate even if unequal when generated by just rules.

These are normative frameworks. Positive economics can predict consequences, not decide the social objective.

Antipoverty policy and effective marginal tax rates

Minimum wages, cash welfare, negative income taxes, earned-income tax credits, and in-kind transfers target poverty through different channels. In-kind provision can address paternalism, insurance failures, or political support but restricts recipient choice.

Benefits that disappear abruptly as earnings rise create a welfare cliff. The effective marginal tax rate includes both explicit taxes and lost benefits; if it exceeds 100 percent, earning more can reduce disposable resources. Gradual phase-outs, earnings subsidies, time limits, or work requirements can reduce this distortion, but each changes coverage, administration, and risk protection. Program design must balance insurance, simplicity, take-up, fiscal cost, and work incentives.

Takeaways

  • Inequality, poverty, and mobility describe different aspects of the distribution.
  • Measurement choices can change both level and trend estimates.
  • Redistribution reflects ethical judgments as well as behavioral responses.
  • Benefit phase-outs should be evaluated together with taxes as one effective budget constraint.
