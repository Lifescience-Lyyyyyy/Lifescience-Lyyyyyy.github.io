Macroeconomics / Chapter 2

Competitive wage determination

Labor demand is derived from production. A profit-maximizing competitive firm hires labor until the value of the marginal product equals the wage. Labor supply reflects the opportunity cost of time and other job attributes. Their interaction determines the equilibrium wage and employment level.

An increase in the wage makes leisure more expensive, creating a substitution effect toward work. It also makes the worker wealthier, creating an income effect toward more leisure. At lower wages the substitution effect often dominates; at sufficiently high wages the income effect may dominate, producing a backward-bending individual labor-supply curve.

Compensating wage differentials

Jobs differ in risk, schedule, location, status, flexibility, and effort. Otherwise similar workers require higher pay to accept an undesirable attribute or may accept lower pay for a desirable one. This compensating differential requires informed workers with meaningful mobility and alternative opportunities. Observed wage gaps therefore do not automatically measure unequal pay for identical work.

Human capital and signaling

Human capital consists of productive knowledge, skill, health, and experience. Education may raise productivity directly; it may also signal pre-existing ability to employers. The distinction matters for policy: subsidizing education raises total productivity under the human-capital mechanism, but a pure signaling race may mainly change credentials.

Skill-biased technical change raises demand for workers who complement new technologies. Trade and specialization can also alter relative labor demand. Ability, effort, family background, networks, and chance are hard to observe, so standard wage regressions leave substantial residual variation and do not establish causality by themselves.

Superstar markets and above-market wages

Superstar income appears when consumers strongly prefer the best performer and technology lets that performer serve a very large market at low marginal cost. Small differences in quality can then generate enormous differences in revenue.

Wages can remain above the competitive level for several reasons:

  • A binding minimum wage creates excess labor supply in a competitive market, but a moderate minimum may raise both wages and employment under monopsony.
  • Unions bargain collectively and may raise member compensation while affecting employment and outsiders.
  • Efficiency wages reduce turnover, attract stronger applicants, improve health, or discourage shirking. Paying more makes job loss costly and can increase effort.

Discrimination and measurement

Discrimination assigns different opportunities to otherwise comparable people because of race, ethnicity, sex, age, appearance, or another protected characteristic. Raw wage gaps mix discrimination with occupation, experience, hours, job amenities, human capital, and prior unequal access to education. Controlling for observed variables narrows the comparison but cannot eliminate omitted-variable bias.

Audit studies that send matched résumés with randomly varied identity signals provide more direct evidence at the hiring stage. Taste-based discrimination can come from employers, customers, coworkers, or institutions. Competition may penalize prejudiced employers, but it need not eliminate discrimination when other market participants are biased or markets are segmented.

Statistical discrimination arises under incomplete information when employers use group averages to predict unobserved individual productivity. Even an accurate group correlation can unfairly penalize an individual and create self-reinforcing incentives. Policies that hide one signal can sometimes increase reliance on noisier group proxies, so outcomes must be evaluated empirically.

Takeaways

  • Wages reflect marginal productivity, labor supply, job amenities, institutions, and bargaining power.
  • Education can build productivity and signal ability; the two mechanisms imply different policies.
  • Minimum wages have different effects under competition and monopsony.
  • Credible discrimination research separates causal treatment from correlated background differences.
