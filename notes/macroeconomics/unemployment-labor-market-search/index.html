Macroeconomics / Chapter 10

Measuring labor-market states

Working-age people are classified as employed, unemployed, or outside the labor force. An unemployed person has no job, is available, and has recently searched under the statistical definition. The unemployment rate and participation rate are

\[u=\frac{U}{E+U}, \qquad \mathrm{LFPR}=\frac{E+U}{\text{working-age population}}.\]

A falling unemployment rate can reflect job finding or labor-force exit, so employment, participation, hours, underemployment, and wages provide complementary evidence.

The labor market is a flow system. People move between employment (E), unemployment (U), and inactivity (I). Even a stable unemployment rate can conceal large simultaneous job loss and job finding. Composition by occupation, industry, age, and duration matters.

Cyclical and structural mechanisms

Keynes emphasized deficient effective demand: when firms cannot sell additional output, lower nominal wages need not restore employment, especially if prices fall too and debt burdens rise. Sticky wages, efficiency wages, unions, minimum wages, search frictions, discrimination, and mismatch can all keep employment below a frictionless competitive benchmark.

Okun’s law is an empirical negative relationship between output growth or the output gap and unemployment. Its coefficient varies across countries and periods because productivity, hours, participation, labor hoarding, and sectoral reallocation differ. Rapid transfer of workers out of agriculture can weaken the simple relationship.

A basic search model

Suppose employed workers lose jobs at rate (s) and unemployed workers find jobs at rate (f). The unemployment stock evolves as

\[\dot U=sE-fU.\]

At steady state, (sE=fU). With labor force (L=E+U),

\[u^*=\frac{s}{s+f}, \qquad \frac{u^*}{1-u^*}=\frac{s}{f}.\]

Higher separation raises unemployment; faster job finding lowers it. Search is productive because it improves matches, so zero unemployment is neither feasible nor necessarily desirable.

A worker accepts an offer when its value exceeds the value of continued search. The reservation wage rises with unemployment benefits, expected offers, and patience, but falls as benefits expire or prospects worsen. Benefits provide valuable insurance and support search quality; they can also lengthen measured unemployment. The net welfare effect depends on financing, liquidity, monitoring, and labor-market conditions.

Two-sided matching

The Diamond–Mortensen–Pissarides framework endogenizes both vacancies and search. A matching function (M(U,V)) converts unemployed workers and vacancies into hires. Market tightness (\theta=V/U) affects the worker job-finding rate and the firm vacancy-filling rate. Wages divide match surplus, while vacancy cost and expected productivity determine job creation.

This framework explains the Beveridge curve—the negative relationship between vacancies and unemployment—and how mismatch or matching efficiency can shift it.

Policy

Information services, job placement, mobility support, retraining, credential recognition, and hiring subsidies can reduce frictional or structural unemployment. Wage-setting reforms affect job creation but also income security and bargaining power. Benefit systems can combine insurance with gradual phase-outs, active search support, and duration dependence. Policy should distinguish cyclical lack of demand from persistent mismatch because their remedies differ.

Takeaways

  • Unemployment is a stock generated by continuous flows between labor-market states.
  • Output, unemployment, participation, and hours must be interpreted together.
  • Steady-state unemployment depends on separation and job-finding rates.
  • Search frictions create both a social cost and the possibility of better worker–job matches.
